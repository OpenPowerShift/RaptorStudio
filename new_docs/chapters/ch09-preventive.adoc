[[ch-pm]]
== Preventive Maintenance: The Careful Commuter

*Time: about 30 minutes.*
*New ideas: scheduled service, refreshing the clock, the trade you
actually made.*

=== You will learn

* What *preventive maintenance* (PM) means to a simulation.
* How a PM schedule is written on a block.
* How to compare "run it until it dies" against "service by the
  calendar" using real numbers.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-standby>> finished.
* The files `models/CarCare0.rbds` (no PM) and `models/CarCare1.rbds`
+
opsmodel:CarCare1[]
  (with PM).

=== The story

Your moped engine wears out. Its lifetime follows a **Weibull** curve
with shape 2.0 and scale 800 h — that shape-2 curve is the classic
"wear-out" signature from <<ch-distributions>>: unlikely to die young,
increasingly likely to die old.

When it does fail, the breakdown repair is ugly: a Lognormal job
averaging about **8 hours**, sometimes much longer (that fat right
tail again).

You have a better idea: every **400 hours**, do a scheduled service.
A service takes only **2 hours**, and afterward the engine runs
*like new*.

Mission: **2000 hours** of commuting. Is the schedule worth it?

=== Two models, one question

Open `CarCare0.rbds`. One block, `Moped_Engine`, between start and end:

[cols="3,2"]
|===
|Failure dist |Weibull(2.0, 800)
|Repair dist |Lognormal, mean ≈ 8 h
|PM |off
|===

Now open `CarCare1.rbds` and select the engine. The PM rows appear:

[cols="4,2",options="header"]
|===
|Setting |Value
|Uses PM |on
|PM interval |400 h
|PM duration |Fixed, 2 h
|PM refresh |like new (clock resets)
|Defer PM until idle |off
|===

[TIP]
====
The interval counts *operating time*, not wall time — same rule as the
failure clock. An engine parked in winter does not need its 400-hour
service while asleep.
====

=== Run both, then argue

Run each file at **200 trials** (set the run count before pressing F5).
The Overview tab summarizes a run at a glance — this one is
`CarCare1.rbds` at 20 trials, so its numbers wobble a little from the
200-trial figures below, exactly as <<ch-trials>> predicts:

image::carcare-results.png[CarCare1 Overview tab: availability, MTBDE, MDT and system health bar]

Typical results at 200 trials:

[cols="5,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |CarCare0 (no PM) |CarCare1 (with PM)
|Availability |about 0.990 |about 0.992
|Mean downtime per stop (MDT) |about 8.1 h |about 3.3 h
|MTBDE |about 969 h |about 423 h
|System failures per run |about 2 |about 5
|===

Read those numbers like a mechanic would:

* Availability barely moves. Both mopeds are up ~99% of the time.
* But look at *how* they spend their down time. Without PM, the moped
  vanishes into rare multi-hour breakdowns. With PM, it visits the
  shop often — but each visit is a quick 2-hour service.
* MTBDE *dropped*. That sounds bad! It is not: more frequent stops,
  most of them planned. The event log shows them plainly —

----
400.000   Block  Moped_Engine   PM_is_due   Scheduled_PM
402.000   Block  Moped_Engine   Good        PM_done
----

[NOTE]
====
This is the single most useful habit in reliability work: never judge
a policy by one number. "Failures per year" went *up* under PM, yet
the commuter's life got better. Always pair frequency with cost per
stop.
====

The trade in one line: PM converts rare, long, surprise outages into
frequent, short, scheduled ones.

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
This model's PM refresh is "like new" — every 400-hour service resets
the wear clock completely, as if the engine were replaced. Real
servicing is rarely that perfect: a mechanic can tighten belts and
change fluids without undoing every hour of wear, especially on parts
the service does not touch. An *imperfect* PM (the block ages a little
even after service) sits between "like new" and "no PM at all," and
is more realistic for most mechanical parts. RAPTOR Studio's PM either
refreshes the block fully or leaves its wear untouched, with nothing in
between, so a real study would need to know which side of "like new"
its own maintenance program actually falls on.
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |Why did MTBDE fall from ~969 h to ~423 h even though PM makes the
engine healthier?
|2 |PM refresh is set to "like new." What would change if the service
only restored half the remaining life?
|3 |The engine's Weibull shape were 1.0 instead of 2.0. Would a
400-hour service still be worth 2 hours of downtime? Think before
simulating.
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. Because PM stops are frequent by design. Every 400 operating hours
   there is a service, so the mean time between *all* stops falls —
   but most of those stops are cheap planned ones instead of rare
   expensive breakdowns.
. Failures would still cluster after long service, so breakdowns would
   creep back between PM visits: MDT and failure severity rise,
   availability gains shrink. "Like new" refresh is what makes a
   fixed interval safe.
. No. Shape 1.0 means Exponential — constant risk, no aging. A
   service cannot reset risk that does not grow; every PM visit buys
   nothing and costs 2 hours. This is why PM pays on wear-out parts
   (shape > 1) and wastes money on chance failures.
====

=== Exercise

Tune the schedule. Try PM intervals of 300, 400, and 500 hours (100+
trials each). For each, record availability and total downtime per
run. Plot downtime against interval. Where does the curve bottom out,
and why does moving the interval *down* eventually make things worse?

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Total downtime per run has a U shape. At very short
intervals the moped spends all its life being serviced (2 h × many
visits). At very long intervals big Lognormal breakdowns dominate.
The bottom sits somewhere near 400–600 h at these settings — and the
exercise's real lesson is that the optimum exists and is findable by
simulation.
====

=== What you learned

* PM is a second clock on a block: interval, duration, refresh rule.
* Scheduled stops are visible as their own log events.
* The right comparison is availability plus the *shape* of downtime —
  not any single headline metric.
