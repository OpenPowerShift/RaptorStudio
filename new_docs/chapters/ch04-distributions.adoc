== Distributions: Same Average, Different Luck

*Time: about 25 minutes.*
*New ideas: failure distributions, why the shape matters, the warranty
trap.*

=== You will learn

* What a failure distribution is and how to read its parameters.
* Why two parts with the *same average life* behave totally differently.
* How to pick a distribution without fooling yourself.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>>, <<ch-redundancy>> and <<ch-voting>> finished.
* The three files `models/Toaster_Fixed.rbds`, `models/Toaster_Expo.rbds`,
+
opsmodel:Toaster_Expo[]
  and `models/Toaster_Weibull.rbds`.

=== The story

Three factories sell heating elements. All three claim: *"average life
about 1000 hours."* You run a toaster warranty service. Your warranty:
**600 hours, full refund**.

Same promise, three contracts. But averages hide the shape of the
risk. One factory builds parts that die like clockwork at exactly
1000 hours. One builds parts where any hour is equally dangerous. One
builds parts that usually live long but sometimes fail in the first
weeks (a bad batch from a dirty oven — it happens).

Question: *which factory costs us the least in refunds?*

=== One new idea: the failure distribution

A **failure distribution** is the dice the engine rolls for each
block's death time. You already met three of them. Now look under the
hood:

image::dist-shapes.svg[Three distributions, same average, different shapes]

[cols="3,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Distribution |Parameters |Character
|Fixed |life |Dies on schedule. Zero surprise.
|Exponential |mean |No memory. Every hour carries the same risk, so many very early failures.
|Weibull |shape, scale |Tunable. Shape > 1 means parts wear out; early failures get rare.
|===

How to read the curves above:

* **Red spike (Fixed):** nothing happens for 1000 hours, then the part
  dies. Within your 600-hour warranty: never.
* **Blue curve (Exponential):** highest risk is right now, at age
  zero. A scary share of parts die young even though the average is
  still 1000 hours.
* **Green curve (Weibull, shape 1.5):** risk starts low, grows with
  age. Fewer early deaths than Exponential.

[TIP]
====
The Exponential's "no memory" property is real physics for electronics:
a transistor does not know how old it is. It is terrible physics for
toaster elements, which clearly wear out. Choose dice that match the
physics, not dice that match the spreadsheet.
====

=== Run the trio

Open each file and press F5. All three use 40 trials and stop at
600 hours. Then raise the run count to 500 for smoother numbers.

[cols="4,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Element |Reliability at 600 h |Refund rate
|Toaster_Fixed |1.00 |0%
|Toaster_Weibull |about 0.67 |about 33%
|Toaster_Expo |about 0.55 |about 45%
|===

About one in ten toasters from the Exponential factory dies before 100
hours — 1 − e^(−100/1000) ≈ 9.5% — while its brochure still says
"average 1000 hours". The Fixed
factory never pays a refund but also never surprises anyone. This gap
between *average* and *early-failure risk* is called the **warranty
trap**, and whole product lines have died in it.

=== Where these names come from

You will meet more dice later (Normal, Gamma, Lognormal, Triangular,
Empirical). Two rules cover most choices:

. If parts fail from *random events, not wear* (electronics, software,
   power cuts): Exponential.
. If parts *wear out* (bearings, belts, heating elements, tires):
   Weibull with shape between 1.5 and 3.

When you have real repair records, the *Empirical* distribution uses
your data directly as the dice — no guessing about families at all.

=== One more idea: the hazard function and the bathtub curve

There is a name for "how risky is this part *right now*, given it has
survived this long": the **hazard function**. It is not the same
question as "what's the average life" — a part can have a long average
life and still be extremely risky at this exact moment.

Three shapes cover almost every real component, and they stack into
the classic **bathtub curve**:

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Life stage |Hazard shape |Matches

|Infant mortality |falling — early failures thin out |Weibull, shape < 1
|Useful life |flat — memoryless, age does not matter |Exponential
|Wear-out |rising — risk grows with age |Weibull, shape > 1
|===

This is *why* the toaster factories' shapes matter so much: Exponential
sits permanently in the flat middle of the bathtub (constant risk,
forever), while Weibull-1.5 starts in the falling-risk zone and slides
into the rising one. Burn-in testing exists to walk a part past the
falling-early-life knee before it ships to you. <<ch-pm>> exists to pull
a wear-out part off the rising edge *before* it climbs too high, by
resetting its clock on a schedule.

The full 22-distribution table — every mean formula and when to reach
for each family — lives in the
xref:../docs/formulas.html#dist-means[Formulas quick reference];
this chapter only needed the three that make the shape argument.

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
Choosing Fixed, Exponential or Weibull for these toasters was a
*modelling decision*, not a measurement. With only a handful of real
field failures, several distribution families can fit the data about
equally well while implying very different early-life risk — exactly
the gap this chapter is built around. A small sample tells you less
about the *shape* of the risk than it feels like it should.
====

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |Two parts have equal mean life. Can one be much worse inside a
short warranty? How?
|2 |Which distribution has no memory?
|3 |Your bearings crack from metal fatigue, which gets worse with
age. Which family fits: Exponential or Weibull with shape 2?
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. Yes. Give one part a distribution with heavy early risk — for
   example Exponential — while the other is Fixed or a wear-out
   Weibull. Equal averages, very different early-failure rates.
. Exponential.
. Weibull with shape 2: risk grows with age, which is what fatigue
   does.
====

=== Exercise

The warranty office proposes cutting the warranty to 400 hours. For
`Toaster_Expo`, compute the expected refund rate by hand:
P(die before t) = 1 − e^(−t/1000). Check your answer against a 500-trial
run.

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
1 − e^(−400/1000) = 1 − 0.670 ≈ 33%. The simulation agrees
(about 0.33 at 500 trials).
====

=== What you learned

* Distributions are dice; parameters set their shape, not just their
  average.
* Equal averages do not mean equal risk, especially early in life.
* Match the dice to the physics: random shocks → Exponential,
  wear-out → Weibull.
