[[ch-capacity]]
== Capacity: The Thirsty Town

*Time: about 30 minutes.*
*New ideas: flow and demand, nominal vs. maximum pipe size,
lost flow, why availability can look perfect while the system
is quietly failing.*

=== You will learn

* How capacity analysis differs from the up/down world of earlier
  chapters.
* What *nominal* and *maximum* flow mean on a link, and how the
  engine fills them.
* The number availability cannot see: *lost flow*.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-hierarchy>> finished.
* The file `models/Water1.rbds`.
+
opsmodel:Water1[]

=== The story

A small town drinks 180 megalitres of water per hour, pumped through
three pipes from the wells to the manifold. Heat wave: the demand is
*more than any two pipes can carry*. The pipes themselves almost
never break — but pipes have sizes, and sizes are numbers, and
numbers can be compared with thirst.

This is a new kind of question. Until now a part was alive or dead.
Now we ask: *how much gets through?*

=== Two numbers per pipe

Open the model. Three pumps fan out from **start**, meet at the
**Manifold**, and flow on to **end**:

image::water-loaded.png[Water1: three pipes into the manifold]

Each pipe (the link from its pump into the Manifold) carries two
numbers:

* **Nominal flow** — what the pipe is *meant* to carry in normal
  service. The town's pipes are rated 30, 30, and 50.
* **Max flow** — the hard ceiling in an emergency. Here 50, 60,
  and 80. All three ceilings together: 190 — barely more than the
  town's 180.

The engine fills every pipe to its *nominal* first. If demand is
still unmet, the leftover is *spilled* into the pipes' headroom, best
pipe first, until the ceilings are reached or the thirst is quenched.
Whatever thirst remains is **lost flow** — and lost flow is money
(<<ch-cost>> will bill for it).

[NOTE]
====
Capacity lives on *links*, not on blocks. A pump that is down simply
makes its pipe unusable; the pipe's size is a property of the
connection. In the `.rbds` file you can see the three numbers on each
link's row (`CapPri`, `nomFlow`, `MaxFlow`); a fresh link drawn in
the GUI starts as "share everything evenly".
====

=== Open the model

Load `models/Water1.rbds`. In **Model → Project Settings** notice
two settings that were never there before:

* **Flow generated: 180** — the demand, offered at the start every hour.
* **Capacity analysis: checked** — tells the engine to track flow,
  not just up/down.

=== Run it

Run **200 trials** of the 8760-hour mission (one year). The summary
still opens with the old friends:

[cols="4,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |Result
|Availability |about 0.9999
|Reliability |about 0.94
|Units produced |about 1 514 000
|Mean flow |about 173 of 180
|===

Availability looks *perfect* — and that is exactly the trap. The
system is "up" whenever any path from start to end exists; with three
pumps, that is nearly always. Meanwhile the town lost about 63 000
megalitres over the year — roughly one day in twenty, drained away
into *yellow* time that availability cannot see.

The new **Capacity** section of the end-of-run report tells the real
story. The system's maximum deliverable flow is 190 when all pumps
run. Watch what one failure costs, straight from the run-1 log:

[cols="3,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Event |Flow now |Lost per hour
|All pumps up |180 |0
|Pump_A down |140 |40
|Pump_B down |130 |50
|Pump_C down |110 |70
|===

The *biggest* pipe causes the *biggest* thirst when it dies. Sizing
redundancy by "any two cover it" hides this: 140, 130, 110 all sound
fine until you remember the town wants 180 every single hour.

[TIP]
====
The health bar now earns its keep: **yellow** means "delivering, but
short". Over the year the model sits in yellow about 13% of the time
and in red essentially never (about 0.007% — all three pumps down at
once). If your study only counts red, it misses the entire drought.
====

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
Flow in this model is instantaneous and has no memory: the moment
Pump_A dies, the town immediately gets less water, and the moment it
is fixed, full flow is immediately restored. Real water systems have a
reservoir that buffers a short outage, and pumps that take minutes to
ramp back to full pressure rather than switching on like a light. A
town with real storage would lose noticeably less than this model's
63 000 megalitres; a model built to size that storage would need to
add it explicitly — capacity analysis here answers "what got through
the pipes this instant," not "what's in the tank."
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |The year's availability is 0.9999. Why is this number nearly
useless for the water company?
|2 |Demand is 180 and the three ceilings sum to 190. Why does the
model still lose water in a year when nothing fails twice?
|3 |Nominal flow is 30 on Pump_A's pipe and its ceiling is 50. When
does the extra 20 get used?
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. Because the town is almost never *down* — a path from wells to
   manifold exists even with a pump out, so the red-light measure
   stays at 0.9999 while the water company is quietly shorted by
   about 4%. Availability counts stoppages; capacity analysis counts
   thirst.
. The 190 total is the sum of the *ceilings*, but demand is offered
   continuously: whenever one pump is down (about 13% of the year
   collectively), the survivors' ceilings of 130–140 sit far below
   the 180 wanted. Each single outage spills 40–70 units per hour,
   and those hours add up to the ~63 000 lost over the year.
. During the spill phase. Every pipe is first filled to its nominal
   (30); only after all nominals are satisfied does the leftover
   demand flow into Pump_A's headroom up to its ceiling of 50.
====

=== Exercise

The council upsizes Pump_C's pipe: nominal 60, max 100 (edit the
`nomFlow`/`MaxFlow` columns of that link in `Water1.rbds`, or ask for
the updated file). Before running: predict the new lost-per-hour when
each single pump is down. Then run and check. Which pump's failure
hurts most now — and is the answer still the biggest pipe?

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
With Pump_C's pipe at nominal 60 / max 100, the ceilings
are 50 + 60 + 100 = 210. One pump down: Pump_A out → 60 + 100 = 160
(lose 20); Pump_B out → 50 + 100 = 150 (lose 30); Pump_C out →
50 + 60 = 110 (lose 70). The biggest pipe still hurts most — upsizing
a dominant pipe does nothing for the case when *it* fails.
====

=== What you learned

* Capacity analysis tracks *how much* flows, not just whether the
  path exists.
* Links carry nominal (soft target) and max (hard ceiling) flow;
  nominal fills first, leftovers spill to the ceiling.
* Lost flow is the drought availability cannot see — a system can
  score 0.9999 and still short the town by four percent.
