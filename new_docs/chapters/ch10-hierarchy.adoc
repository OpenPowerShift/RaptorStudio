[[ch-hierarchy]]
== Hierarchies: The Delivery Van, Tidied Up

*Time: about 25 minutes.*
*New ideas: hierarchies (boxes inside boxes), the In/Out doors,
why structure is not behaviour.*

=== You will learn

* What a *hierarchy* is and when a diagram needs one.
* How flow crosses a hierarchy's boundary.
* The one rule every modeler must memorize: collapsing changes
  the picture, never the numbers.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-pm>> finished.
* The files `models/Van1.rbds` and `models/VanFlat.rbds`.
+
opsmodel:VanFlat[]

=== The story

A delivery van has an engine problem and a tire problem. Its diagram
so far would be six blocks on one flat sheet: Fuel_Pump and
Engine_Core in a row, then four tires voting "at least 3 of 4".
<<ch-voting>> taught you the vote; this chapter is about *paperwork*.

Real systems do not live on one sheet. A van engineer draws the
engine on one page, the chassis on another, and the whole van on a
cover page. RAPTOR Studio has exactly this idea: the *hierarchy*, a
box that hides a whole sub-diagram inside itself.

The four tires fan out from the In marker and rejoin at a vote node —

[TIP]
====
Every hierarchy has two special markers. *In* is the door where flow
enters; *Out* is where it leaves. Anything between them is invisible
from outside — the parent diagram only sees one oval.
====

The oval itself has two readings. Its *body* colour is the hierarchy's
own status: green when nothing is lost, yellow when it still works but
something inside has failed, red when the subsystem is down, plus blue
for standby, brown for idle and orange for preventive maintenance. The
*two downward triangles in the middle* are Raptor's Descendant Status
Indicator (DSI): the worst status of the hierarchies inside this one.
Green body with a yellow or red DSI means this subsystem still works but
something deeper is degraded or down; a red body means the subsystem
itself is down. The name sits inside the oval at rest; while a run is
animated, stepped or shown with its triangles, it moves under the oval, as
in Raptor, so the triangles keep the middle of the body to themselves.

While you animate or step a run, both are live. After an ordinary run
(with Weak Link analysis on), the triangles show the share of time, over
every run, that the inside was working, partly failed, on standby or
down. Point at the oval and it says which.

=== The experiment that proves the point

Here is the claim worth testing: **the hierarchy changed nothing
physical**. `VanFlat.rbds` is the same six blocks with no ovals at all.

Run both files at **200 trials**:

[cols="4,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |Van1 (hierarchical) |VanFlat (flat)
|Availability |about 0.987 |about 0.987
|MTBDE |about 421 h |about 421 h
|MDT |about 4.6 h |about 4.6 h
|===

Identical — digit for digit. The engine does not know an oval from a
straight line. A hierarchy is pure *organisation*: it exists so a
human can draw, read, and reuse big diagrams without a wall-sized
sheet.

So when does a real study need them?

* The diagram has more than a screenful of blocks.
* Whole subsystems repeat (four wheels, each its own brake-and-tire
  group — draw once, place four ovals).
* Different teams own different parts and want their own page.

[NOTE]
====
Collapsing and uncollapsing are safe *because* they preserve results.
That is also why they are dangerous nowhere else: if someone tells you
a subsystem was "simplified during collapse", ask for both runs'
numbers. They must match.
====

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
Van1 and VanFlat matched digit for digit because nothing in this van
needs anything more than a *block* to die for the system to notice —
and block deaths do cross a hierarchy boundary cleanly. A *node* going
Bad (a lost vote, a stalled switchover) is a different story: that
status change does not propagate out through a hierarchy's Out marker
the way a block failure does. Keep any vote or switch logic you care
about, and the parts that react to it, on the same hierarchy level —
splitting them across a boundary is a real engine limit, not just a
drawing choice.
====

=== Checking one part on its own

[NOTE]
====
This section is about running one hierarchy on its own. Its results are
labelled as an isolated run everywhere they appear, and are never
presented as whole-model results. To see a hierarchy's figures inside the
whole model, run the whole model and read the weak-link results.
====

When you are building a big model one part at a time, you often want to
know how *just the tires* behave before the rest of the van exists. Pick
the hierarchy (the Model Explorer or the canvas), choose **Extract
Hierarchy…** on the Model ribbon, and RAPTOR Studio builds a small
standalone model: the hierarchy's contents become the whole diagram, its
In door becomes the Start and its Out door the End. You can run it, save
it. From the command line the same
thing is:

[source,console]
----
raptor extract Van1.rbds --hierarchy Tires_sub -o tires.rbd
raptor run Van1.rbds --hierarchy Tires_sub
----

**What the input means.** The extract assumes the hierarchy's input is
*always available*. Anything upstream of the door is outside and ignored,
and every result says so.

**What is lost at the boundary.** Some things inside a hierarchy are tied
to things outside it. RAPTOR Studio scans for three kinds, tells you which
it found, and lets you pick what to do about each:

[cols="1,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Kind |What it is |Choices (the first is the default)

|Outside dependencies
|A block inside follows a block, node, event or hierarchy outside, or
follows the whole system.
|`up`: the outside master is assumed always up (optimistic: no induced
idling or failure from outside). `system`: "the system" becomes "this
hierarchy" (right when the hierarchy is what brings the system down,
optimistic otherwise). `refuse`: stop and list them.

|Shared pools and resources
|A spare pool or resource used by blocks inside **and** outside.
|`full`: the extract gets the whole pool (optimistic: no competition).
`share`: stock and units are cut to the share of users inside, rounded
down, at least 1 (an approximation of a fair split, not real contention).
`set`: you give the numbers. `refuse`.

|Random-number streams
|Blocks inside draw from a stream that blocks outside also use. This is
the normal case: most blocks use the shared system stream.
|`keep`: the stream numbers stay. The isolated run is a valid independent
estimate, but it cannot repeat the draws the hierarchy saw inside the
whole model. `separate`: each such block gets its own unused stream.
|===

[IMPORTANT]
====
No choice can make an isolated run reproduce the numbers the hierarchy
produced inside the whole model. Shared streams are consumed in
model-wide event order, and that order is gone once the rest is removed;
the results differ by statistical noise, never by a bias you cannot
explain. An "always down" choice is deliberately not offered: a block
that is permanently idle or failed is never what someone checking a part
of a model wants.
====

Costs, weak-link and capacity results describe the extract only. A model
that ends after N system failures (or N phase cycles) now counts the
hierarchy's failures, not the system's. If the hierarchy is itself
phased, its own Cut or Linked phases are ignored in the isolated run, and
you are warned.

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |A hierarchy's Out marker fails like a block. What happens to the
parent diagram?
|2 |In VanFlat the tires' vote sits directly in the main flow. Where
did it go in Van1?
|3 |You move a repairable block from inside a hierarchy to the top
level without touching its dice. Which numbers change?
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. Nothing directly — but if flow can no longer pass through the
   hierarchy (its interior cannot reach the Out marker), the parent
   loses that path exactly as if an ordinary block had died. The oval
   is only a window; failures inside are what matter.
. Nowhere visible from the home level — it lives inside Tires_sub,
   behind the In/Out doors. The parent shows one oval where the flat
   model shows four blocks and a node.
. None. Same dice in the same streams give the same event log;
   availability, MTBDE, MDT all stay put. Only the picture changes —
   which is exactly the chapter's point.
====

=== Exercise

Give the van a spare wheel. Inside `Tires_sub`, add a fifth block
`Tire_Spare` (Exponential, mean 800 h, Fixed 2 h repair) feeding the
same vote node, and raise the vote to n = 5 while keeping k = 3.
Before running: does availability rise or fall? Then run 100 trials
and compare against the table above.

[NOTE]
====
Adding an element *into* a hierarchy is the one placement task with a
trick to it, so here is the whole sequence. The hierarchy's id is the
number after the colon in its name in the *Model Explorer* tree
(`RBDHier(20)`), or read the `Hierarchies` table that
`describe_model(detail='full')` prints.

. Get inside. Double-click the `Tires_sub` oval, or select the `Tires_sub`
  node in the *Model Explorer* and expand it.
. Place the block. *Model → Add Block*, click inside the oval, then
  right-click the canvas to leave the tool. Rename it `Tire_Spare` and set
  Exponential mean 800, Fixed repair 2 in the *Inspector* or *Data Tables*.
. Wire it in. *Model → Connect*, then click `Tire_Spare` and then the
  `Tires_vote` node. A block may take only one link in and one link out;
  `Tires_vote` is the node that is allowed many of each.
. Raise the vote. `Tires_vote` now has five incoming links, so its `n`
  reads 5 on its own. Set `k` to 3 — the vote needs three working tires,
  not five.
. Check. Press *F7*. A new block that never fails because nothing
  reaches it, or a node whose `n` disagrees with its real links, is
  reported here rather than at simulation time.

The same three things are what an assistant needs, and they are the
fiddly part: `add_block(parent=20, ...)` puts the block inside the oval,
`connect` attaches it to the vote, and `k` is then set explicitly because
`k` is never inferred.
====

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Availability rises. With five tires and "need 3", the van
tolerates two flat tires instead of one; expected availability climbs
from about 0.987 toward roughly 0.99+ at 100 trials. The spare wheel
is cheap insurance because the vote still forgives two losses.
====

=== What you learned

* A hierarchy is a named box hiding a sub-diagram behind In/Out doors.
* Descending and ascending are navigation, never simulation changes.
* Structure and behaviour are independent: flattening must reproduce
  every number exactly.
