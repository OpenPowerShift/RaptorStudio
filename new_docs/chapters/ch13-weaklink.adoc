[[ch-weaklink]]
== Weak Links: Blame the Right Part

*Time: about 30 minutes.*
*New ideas: weak-link analysis, frequency vs. importance, fixing the
weak link and meeting the next one.*

=== You will learn

* Why the part that fails most is often *not* the part that hurts most.
* How to find the true weak link: who is to blame when the system
  actually stops?
* What happens after you fix it (hint: a new weak link volunteers).

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-hierarchy>> finished.
* The file `models/VanFlat.rbds` and its event log habit from <<ch-redundancy>>.
+
opsmodel:VanFlat[]

=== The story

The van's mechanic wants attention paid to the tires — "they fail
constantly!" The dispatcher disagrees: "When did a tire ever stop a
delivery?" Both are holding real data. Only simulation can settle
*whose* data matters.

=== Busy is not the same as harmful

The van has two kinds of parts. The four tires fail often — but the
3-of-4 vote from <<ch-voting>> forgives any single flat, so the van
limps on, marked *yellow*. The fuel pump and the engine core sit in
the series chain: when either dies, the delivery dies with it.

So we expect two different league tables:

* a *frequency* table — who fails most (the tires, comfortably);
* an *importance* table — who causes stoppages (spoiler: not the tires).

The weak link is the top of the *importance* table. Money spent
anywhere else buys almost nothing.

=== Open the model

Load `models/VanFlat.rbds`. In **Model → Project Settings**, tick
**Weak Link analysis** and Apply. That one checkbox tells the engine
to keep a per-part timesheet: hours running, hours under repair, per
trial.

=== Run it

Run **200 trials** of the 2000-hour mission and open one trial's
report. Each part's timesheet, averaged over the trials:

[cols="3,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Part |Hours running |Hours in repair
|Fuel_Pump |about 1 984 |about 15.7
|Engine_Core |about 1 990 |about 10.5
|each Tire (×4) |about 1 995 |about 4.9
|===

The tires *combined* rack up more repair time than either series part
— the mechanic's data is honest. Now the importance table: read 200
event logs (<<ch-redundancy>>'s skill) and ask, at every moment the system
went down (red), *which part was down?*

[cols="3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Blamed for a stoppage (red) |Share
|Fuel_Pump |about 68%
|Engine_Core |about 31%
|any Tire |about 1%
|===

And the same count for yellow (degraded but drivable) moments: the
tires own essentially all of them — about 1 900 flat-tire events
across the trials, and the van kept driving through nearly every one.

That is the whole trick of weak-link thinking in one table. The tires
are the busiest parts in the van and almost the *least* important.
The fuel pump fails a third as often and causes two-thirds of the
stoppages.

[TIP]
====
A part's importance is set by two dials: how often it fails, and
what its failure *costs the system*. Series parts have maximal cost —
any failure is a stoppage. Redundant parts have small cost — their
failures are absorbed. When you budget improvements, multiply
frequency by cost, and look at the product, not the frequency.
====

=== Let the colours do the reading

**Results ▸ Show on Canvas ▸ Weak Links** paints each block by its availability
against two thresholds, Green ≥ 0.95 and Red < 0.90 unless you change
them. In a well-designed system every block can sit above 0.95, so
everything comes out Green. Press **Spread** beside it: it
moves them to the quartiles of the result set's averages, so the worst quarter
of the blocks turns Red and the best quarter Green. It ranks the blocks
against each other, not against an absolute target, so read it as
"where to look first".

=== Fix the weak link, meet the next one

The fuel pump is guilty. Upgrade it: change `Fuel_Pump`'s failure
mean from 500 to 1500 hours (F Param 1 in the Data Tables), run 200
trials again, and compare:

[cols="4,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |Before |After
|Stoppages per trial |about 5.7 |about 3.4
|MTBDE |about 420 h |about 755 h
|Availability |about 0.987 |about 0.992
|===

Real improvement — MTBDE up four-fifths. But look again at the
importance table: the fuel pump's share collapsed, and *Engine_Core*
inherited the crown (now roughly half of all stoppages). Systems do
not run out of weak links; they promote new ones. Tuning is a loop:
measure, fix the leader, re-measure — exactly the loop the final
chapter's data centre will put to work.

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
The importance table's whole logic — "redundant parts divide their
cost, series parts multiply it" — assumes each part's failure is a
private event. If a pothole blows two tires at once, or a single bad
fuel batch stresses the pump and the engine core together, the real
importance ranking shifts in ways this per-part timesheet cannot see:
shared causes make redundant-looking parts behave more like series
parts. Weak-link analysis tells you where to spend money *given* the
independence assumption; it is silent about whether that assumption
holds.
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |The tires accumulate more total repair hours than the fuel pump.
Why do they cause almost no stoppages?
|2 |You replace the 3-of-4 tire vote with 2-of-4 (two flats tolerated).
What happens to the tires' share of the importance table?
|3 |The fuel pump upgrade improved MTBDE by 80%, not the 200% its
failure-rate improvement suggests. Where did the missing
improvement go?
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. The 3-of-4 vote absorbs any single flat: a tire failure degrades
   the system (yellow) but almost never stops it. Repair *hours* and
   stoppage *blame* are different tables — the vote is what decouples
   them.
. The tires' share of *yellow* time shrinks further, but it was
   already near zero on the importance table. Stoppage blame stays
   with the series pair — redundancy upgrades protect the part, not
   the weak link.
. The stoppages the pump no longer causes were only part of the
   total; the engine core's stoppages remain untouched. Improvement
   is bounded by the *sum* of all causes, so removing one cause of
   68% cannot yield 200% — and the engine's share grows as the
   pump's shrinks.
====

=== Exercise

Without running anything: the engine core now causes about half of
stoppages. Predict the MTBDE if Engine_Core's Weibull mean doubles
(shape stays 2, mean 1000 → 2000; F Param 2 in the Data Tables).
Then run 200 trials and check yourself. Did the *next* weak link
already appear?

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Red events fall from about 3.4 to about 2.1 per trial and
MTBDE roughly doubles, to about 1 170 h (availability ≈ 0.995). The
prediction "engine stoppages halve" is close — slightly optimistic,
since doubling a wear-out part's mean pushes many failures past the
mission's end. And yes, a weak link always volunteers: with both
upgrades in place the fuel pump is the top cause again (about 65% of
what little remains).
====

=== Which part would you fix first?

Weak links rank parts by how much time they cost. There is a second
question, and it is the one budgets are actually spent on: *if I could
make one part better, which one buys the most?*

Switch on **Run ▸ Analysis ▸ Importance measures** (leave it on
**Both**) and run the same 200 trials. That costs twelve more batches,
one per block per variant, so it takes a few minutes — set **Cores**
above 1 first. Open **Results ▸ Final Results ▸ Importance**:

[cols="4,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Block |Share of the downtime |RRW

|Fuel_Pump |about 56% |about 2.3
|Engine_Core |about 37% |about 1.6
|each tire |about 0% |about 1
|===

Two parts hold 94% of the van's downtime between them. That matches the
weak-link chapter's story, and it is the same answer reached a different
way: weak links read the *state timesheet*, importance reads *what
happens to the system when this block is taken out of the picture*.

The tires are the interesting row. Their share is a hair below zero,
which looks like nonsense until you know what the number is made of:
each measurement is two extra batches of simulated futures, and those
draw their own random numbers, so a part that does not gate the system
lands either side of zero on noise. **At or below zero means "no
measurable effect", not "fixing this makes it worse".** That is why the
column has a standard error beside it, and why the page ranks by share
of downtime rather than by Birnbaum — in a series van Birnbaum reads
about 0.99 for *every* part, which tells you nothing about which to
fix.

Change **Measured on** to *Reliability* and the ranking can move: a part
can be cheap in downtime and expensive in failed missions. Which of the
two you want is a question about your van, not about the arithmetic.

=== What you learned

* Weak-link analysis separates *busy* parts from *important* parts.
* Importance = failure frequency × what the failure does to the
  system; series position multiplies, redundancy divides.
* Fixing a weak link promotes the runner-up — improvement is a loop,
  not a destination.
