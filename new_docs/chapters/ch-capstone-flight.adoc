[[ch-capstone-flight]]
== Capstone: The Flight, Phase by Phase (An Open-Ended Brief)

*Time: about 60–90 minutes.*
*New idea: none. Every mechanism below is one you already own — twin
engines and a shared limit (<<ch-capacity>>), a cold-start backup
(<<ch-standby>>), one system quietly wired to another
(<<ch-dependency>>), a one-shot go/no-go check (<<ch-events>>), a
mission with stages (<<ch-phasing>>), a bill (<<ch-cost>>). What is
new is doing it yourself, from a written brief, with nobody telling
you which tool to reach for.*

=== You will learn

* How to read a plain-English systems description and recognize which
  chapter's mechanism each sentence is quietly asking for.
* How to defend a modelling choice when two subsystems you built
  separately turn out to interact in a way the brief never spelled out.
* Why "the redundancy is on the canvas" and "the redundancy works" are
  different claims — and why simulation, not inspection, is how you
  tell them apart.

=== You need

* Every chapter so far. This capstone leans hardest on
  <<ch-standby>>, <<ch-dependency>>, <<ch-events>>, <<ch-phasing>>,
  <<ch-capacity>>, and <<ch-cost>>, in no particular order — the brief
  below does not tell you which one applies where.
* A blank model. Start `File → New` and build from the brief. (A
  reference solution exists — `models/Flight1.rbds` — but it is
  deliberately not linked here. Build first; the link is in
  <<flight-worked-answer>>, below, once you have numbers of your own
  to compare it against.)

=== The brief

You are the reliability engineer for a short regional hop, gate to
gate. Two hours, four phases: **Taxi** (12 minutes), **Climb** (18
minutes), **Cruise** (78 minutes), **Approach** (12 minutes). Build
the mission as one continuous run — this is a single flight, not a
shift that repeats.

Before pushback, dispatch runs a weather and load check. It clears the
flight to depart 98% of the time; the other 2% the flight is scrubbed
at the gate before anything else happens.

Propulsion is two engines, feeding a common thrust requirement. The
flight plan calls for 150 units of combined thrust; each engine is
rated for 75 nominal but can be pushed to 100 in an emergency. Both
engines wear out — Weibull, shape 2.0, characteristic life 8 operating
hours — and work hardest in **Climb** (call it ×1.8 the baseline wear
rate), easiest in **Cruise** (×0.6), with **Taxi** and **Approach** in
between (×1.0 and ×1.4). A dead engine cannot be restarted in flight.

Hydraulic System 1 is driven off Engine 1's accessory gearbox —
Exponential, mean 50 hours, and a crew can reset it to a backup
circuit in about 3 minutes when it acts up.

Electrical power comes from an engine-driven Main Generator
(Weibull, shape 2.0, characteristic life 10 hours, no in-flight
repair) backed by an Auxiliary Power Unit that sits cold and
unstressed until it is needed, then spins up in about 72 seconds
(Exponential once running, mean 15 hours, no in-flight repair
either). The Avionics Suite downstream of the bus fails on its own
schedule too — Exponential, mean 40 hours, about a 72-second
circuit-breaker reset when it trips.

Cost analysis is on: $50 000 for every hour the aircraft is fully
grounded, $2 000 for every unit of thrust the engines fall short of
the 150-unit plan, for every hour the shortfall lasts.

=== Your task

Build it. Some starting questions, not a checklist to tick in order:

* Which of <<ch-voting>>'s vote and <<ch-capacity>>'s manifold does
  "two engines, a combined thrust requirement, emergency headroom"
  actually describe? They are not the same mechanism, and they do not
  produce the same story when one engine dies.
* The dispatch check happens once, at the gate, with no operating
  clock. What kind of element in this engine matches that description,
  and how is it different from a block?
* Which failures in the brief can a crew address in the air, and which
  cannot? The brief tells you this for every system — assigning a
  repair distribution (or none) to each block *is* the decision, even
  if you make it without noticing you made it.
* Read the hydraulic paragraph again. Is anything in this model
  described as depending on anything else?
* Set the mission to 2000 trials before you trust a single number —
  <<ch-trials>>'s own rule, and this chapter's headline metric is a
  coin-flip one.

[TIP]
====
This is a go/no-go mission, not a system that cycles up and down for
a shift. Which of this book's metrics — Availability or Reliability —
answers "did the flight complete," and which Project Settings field
sets that as the headline?
====

=== Before you run it

Write down two numbers before you press F5 for real:

. Your guess at Reliability, as a percentage, for 2000 trials.
. A prediction: does losing Engine 1 alone cost the flight the same
  as losing Engine 2 alone, or a different amount? Say why, in one
  sentence, before you have data.

Keep both. You will want them in a few minutes.

=== Check your build

A few low-spoiler sanity numbers, not the full answer:

* Weather scrubs should land near 2% of your 2000 runs. If you see
  roughly 98% instead, you have the Bernoulli success/failure
  convention backwards — <<ch-events>>'s FinaleShow1 note applies
  here too: the draw equal to the failure value is the one that stops
  the block, and it is easy to wire that upside down.
* With both engines healthy, most of your mission time should read
  Green. If you see heavy Yellow time even with nothing failed, check
  your thrust numbers against the brief's 150-unit demand — a mistyped
  capacity value shows up as a permanent, not occasional, shortfall.
* If Reliability comes back at 0.00 or 1.00 across all 2000 trials,
  something upstream is wired wrong (usually the dispatch event, or a
  link direction) — not a single block in this brief is that certain
  or that safe on its own.

Get real numbers before reading on.

=== One worked answer

There is no single correct file for an open brief like this one — two
careful readers can make different, equally defensible choices about
the questions in "Your task" and land on different numbers. What
follows is *one* valid build, not *the* answer key. Use it to check
whether your numbers are in the same neighborhood, and more
importantly, whether the *shape* of your result — which failures cost
the flight and which merely cost money — matches what the brief
actually described.

opsmodel:Flight1[]

image::flight1-loaded.png[Flight1 loaded: dispatch event, twin engines into a thrust manifold, hydraulic pump, generator cold-standby pair, avionics]

Fourteen elements: one dispatch event, two engines feeding a
capacity-limited thrust manifold, a dependent hydraulic pump, a
cold-standby generator pair, and an avionics block, strung through
four fixed-length phases.

Run 2000 trials:

image::flight1-results.png[Flight1 Overview tab: 96.1% availability, 82.2% reliability, 2.9% degraded, 3.9% down]

[cols="3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |This build
|Reliability |about 82%
|Availability |about 96.1%
|Degraded (Yellow) time |about 2.9%
|Fully down (Red) time |about 3.9%
|Weather scrubs |about 1.9% of flights
|===

Now the question this chapter was actually built to ask. Split the
2000 runs by which engine failed:

[cols="3,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Which engine failed |Share of all flights |Still completed the mission
|Engine 1 alone |about 4.6% |0% — every single one
|Engine 2 alone |about 4.5% |about 88%
|Both |about 0.3% |0%
|Neither |about 90.5% |about 86%
|===

Check your "before you run it" prediction now. If you guessed "the
same amount either way," the capacity manifold's own logic agrees with
you — 100 units from one healthy engine against a 150-unit plan is the
same 50-unit shortfall no matter which engine survives. The actual
numbers disagree, completely. Read one Engine-1-alone run's event log
to see why:

----
0.500000   Block   Engine_1        Failed    Operating_time=0.565413
0.565413   Block   Engine_1        Done      No_repair
0.565413   Block   Hydraulic_Pump  Idle      Dependence
0.565413   Node    Thrust_Manifold Degraded  1-1/2_df5_ur6
0.565413   System  State_change    Red       _d1
----

The manifold does exactly what it was built to do — `Degraded`, not
`Bad`, because one engine is still delivering. But `Hydraulic_Pump`
goes `Idle` on the same event, tagged `Dependence`, and *that* is what
drags the whole system to `Red`. The brief's own sentence — "Hydraulic
System 1 is driven off Engine 1's accessory gearbox" — was the
dependency the whole time. It does not appear as a warning label. It
reads exactly like the rest of the systems description, and it costs
the flight in series, with no capacity margin or vote left to forgive
it, the moment Engine 1 specifically is the one that dies.

If your own file shows this same asymmetry, you caught the gearbox
sentence and modelled it. If your two engines behave symmetrically,
you built a real, coherent aircraft — just not quite this one, and
that is a legitimate outcome for an open brief. Go check which is
true of your file before reading further: is Engine 1 special in your
model, the way the brief's prose says it should be?

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
Two things worth stating plainly rather than discovering by surprise.
First, Reliability here is zero-tolerance, exactly like every earlier
reliability chapter: the Main-Generator-fails-APU-recovers case still
counts as a failed mission even though the bus is back up in 72
seconds and the flight finishes the leg without another incident — a
transient Red excursion is still a Red excursion. Second, the
dependency mechanic only supports the all-or-nothing case this book
has shown since <<ch-dependency>> (100% Idle) — a real hydraulic
crossfeed would likely degrade Hydraulic System 1 partially, not idle
it completely, so this brief's numbers are as pessimistic on that
point as the engine can currently express. And as with every earlier
capstone: every failure here is drawn independently, the dollar
figures are teaching numbers chosen to make the arithmetic legible,
not real aircraft economics, and no fault-tree detail from an actual
airworthiness process is anywhere in this twelve-element model.
====

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
This chapter has no single answer key — two honest builds can
legitimately disagree. What follows discusses the reference build's
own choices, not a scorecard for yours.

. Two real examples from the reference build: the engines'
   characteristic life (8 operating hours) and the generator's
   bus-transfer delay (72 seconds) both came from nowhere in the
   brief's prose — they were picked to make the mechanism visible at
   2000 trials, the same "compress the numbers" move every earlier
   capstone has made openly. A shorter characteristic life pushes
   Reliability down; a shorter transfer delay nudges it up (fewer
   Red ticks long enough to matter, though this metric is
   zero-tolerance regardless of duration — see the Limits box).
   Your own file almost certainly has at least one number in the same
   position: something the brief left to your judgement.
. `redcost` prices the hours the *whole aircraft* is grounded — a
   fixed, lump consequence, modelled as an hourly rate the way every
   earlier capstone has done. `lostcost` prices something continuous
   and partial: thrust actually missing from the 150-unit plan,
   which can happen *without* the aircraft being fully down at all
   (one engine out, still flying, still short). One flight can rack
   up either cost alone, or both at once — they are not
   interchangeable, and collapsing them into one rate would erase
   the very distinction <<ch-capacity>> exists to teach.
. Reliability should rise, and the Engine-1/Engine-2 asymmetry in the
   worked-answer table should vanish: with the dependency gone,
   losing either engine alone becomes an ordinary capacity shortfall
   — Yellow, a `lostcost` bill, and (usually) a completed flight,
   for both engines symmetrically. The reference build's ~82% is a
   pessimistic number *because* of the gearbox sentence; remove it
   and the true redundancy the manifold was built to provide finally
   shows up in the numbers.
. `SbStress` (0.0 in the reference build, and a new block's
   default). At 1.0, the APU would age at full rate while sitting
   parked and unused — a *hot* standby, not the cold one the brief
   actually described ("sits cold and unstressed until it is
   needed"). The model would quietly become less realistic: an APU
   that has been wearing out the whole flight even though it never
   ran would fail more often than a real cold-stowed unit ever would.
====

=== Exercise

The airline wants one more nine of reliability, without adding any new
redundancy — no third engine, no second APU. You have three honest,
single-number levers already on the table: the engines' characteristic
life, the hydraulic pump's dependency, and the generator's bus-transfer
delay. Pick one, write one sentence defending why you picked it over
the other two, change only that number, and run 2000 trials. Did you
clear 90%? If not, name what is still costing you reliability that a
single-number change on your chosen lever could never reach.

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Of the three levers, the dependency is the one that costs
nothing physically to fix (rewiring a hydraulic crossfeed line is a
paperwork and routing change, not a heavier engine or a faster
switch) and it is also the single biggest lever on the table — the
worked answer's own numbers show Engine-1 losses going from 0%
survival to something close to Engine-2's ~88% the moment the
dependency is removed, which alone is worth several points of
Reliability. Characteristic life and switch delay both move the
number too, but neither erases an entire failure mode the way
finding and cutting one bad dependency does — the same lesson
<<ch-dependency>> taught with two blocks, now worth defending with
fourteen.
====

=== What you learned

* A brief, not a click-path, is what reliability work actually looks
  like — <<ch-phasing>> through <<ch-cost>>'s techniques do not
  announce themselves; a plain sentence in a systems description is
  the only signal you get.
* Two subsystems that look independent on the canvas can be wired
  together by one sentence in a systems description, and that single
  sentence can silently cancel out redundancy you built somewhere else
  entirely — <<ch-dependency>>'s own lesson, this time costing you a
  weak-link investigation to notice instead of being handed to you on
  page one.
* "Defend the answer" means checking that your numbers are the ones
  *your own* modelling choices predict — not that they match this
  chapter's table. Two careful engineers can read the same brief and
  ship two different, equally honest aircraft.

That is the whole toolbox, spent on one last machine with nobody
holding your hand. Go point it at something that matters to you.
