== Capstone: The Line That Paid for Its Spares

*Time: about 40 minutes.*
*New ideas: finding your own weak links on a pure series chain, the
RSO switch that turns "has spares" into "has a spares plan," and
reading a cost comparison instead of an availability comparison.*

=== You will learn

* Why a pure series chain collapses ch13's *frequency* table and
  *importance* table into one — and how to use that shortcut.
* What "Routine Spare Ordering" actually does, and why <<ch-sparing>>'s
  Taco Truck never got to show you the other half of it.
* How to ask "did this investment pay for itself," using a real
  Grand_Total, not just an Availability number.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-cost>> finished — this chapter
  leans on series links, Weibull wear-out (<<ch-distributions>>),
  Custom sparing (<<ch-sparing>>), preventive maintenance (<<ch-pm>>),
  and cost analysis (<<ch-cost>>).
* The files `models/ProdLine0.rbds` (as commissioned) and
  `models/ProdLine1.rbds` (after the ops review).
+

=== The story

A bottling line: rinse, fill, cap, inspect the cap, label, date-code,
form the carton, pack the case, seal it, inspect it, palletize, wrap,
ship. Fourteen stations, bolted together in one straight chain.
<<ch-first-model>>'s oldest rule, scaled up fourteen times: one broken
link and the whole line stops.

image::prodline-loaded.png[ProdLine1 loaded: fourteen stations in a straight series chain]

The plant manager has a budget question, not an engineering one: the
line carries a modest spares shelf and a maintenance contract that
together cost real money every year. Is any of it worth what it
costs?

=== Find your own weak link

Open `models/ProdLine0.rbds` — the line exactly as it was commissioned,
no changes yet. This time nobody hands you a blame table the way
<<ch-weaklink>> will. Work it out yourself, and notice *why* it is
easier here than it will be there: this line is pure series, with no
vote to forgive anything. On a system like that, "how often does a
part fail" and "how much does its failure hurt" are the *same
number* — every failure, anywhere, stops the whole line. <<ch-weaklink>>
needs two separate tables because VanFlat's tires fail constantly but
mean almost nothing; this line has no tires. One table is enough.

Run 200 trials, open **Data Tables → Sparing**, and read
`Spares_Utilization`'s `AveUsed` column — for a block still on
Infinite sparing, it is simply a per-run failure count. Three stations
stand well clear of the rest:

[cols="3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Station |Failures per run
|Capper |about 9.1
|Filler |about 7.6
|Case_Packer |about 7.0
|===

Every other station sits under 3. All three leaders share the same
signature — a Weibull curve with shape well above 1, <<ch-distributions>>'s
wear-out shape. That is not a coincidence you need statistics to see;
it is the whole reason `ProdLine1` only ever touches these three
stations.

=== One new idea: RSO turns "has spares" into "has a plan"

<<ch-sparing>>'s Taco Truck kept three propane tanks on a shelf that
nobody ever restocked — when the fourth failure came, the truck closed
for the season. That chapter's whole point was showing you what
happens *without* a resupply plan. Open `ProdLine0.rbds` and select
**Filler**: it is Custom-spared, stock 3, exactly like Propane_Tank,
right down to the field that made the truck's ending permanent —
`RSO=0`, no routine spare ordering. Capper carries the same setup,
stock 2.

Now open `ProdLine1.rbds` and select the same block:

[cols="3,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Setting |ProdLine0 |ProdLine1
|Sparing type |Custom |Custom
|Stock |3 |3
|Routine Spare Ordering (RSO) |off |*on*
|Resupply interval |— |every 720 h (30 days)
|Emergency Spare Ordering (ESO) |off |*on*
|Emergency lead time |— |48 h
|===

`RSO=1` turns the shelf into a standing purchase order: a fresh tank
arrives every 30 days whether or not one was used, and if the shelf
ever actually runs dry anyway, an emergency shipment lands in 48
hours instead of never arriving at all. Case_Packer gets the third
piece of the review: scheduled PM every 600 hours
(<<ch-pm>>'s pattern exactly), refreshing it before its Weibull clock
ever gets the chance to wear it out.

No other station changes between the two files.

=== Run both

Run **200 trials** of the 8 760-hour year on each file:

[cols="4,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |ProdLine0 |ProdLine1
|Availability |about 93.5% |about *96.5%*
|MDT |about 11.4 h |about 5.3 h
|System failures per run |about 50 |about 59
|===

image::prodline1-results.png[ProdLine1 Overview tab: 96.5% availability, MDT 5.3 hours]

Read that middle row carefully before the cost table: failures per
run went *up* after the fix, not down. A line that is actually running
is a line that can fail again; a line stuck dead on its fourth Capper
failure, like ProdLine0 spends real time being, cannot rack up a fifth.
Count alone would tell you the wrong story — MDT is the number that
says each of those failures now costs less than half as much time.

Now the number the plant manager actually asked about. Both files
carry a System red cost of $8 000 per down-hour
(<<ch-cost>>'s field, same one DC1/DC2 used):

[cols="4,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Cost_Summary line |ProdLine0 |ProdLine1
|Blocks (spares + PM) |about $35 000 |about $52 000
|SystemDownCost |about $4 561 000 |about $2 487 000
|*Grand_Total* |about *$4 596 000* |about *$2 540 000*
|===

The review spent about $17 000 a year more on parts and maintenance.
That $17 000 bought back about $2.07 million a year in avoided
downtime. <<ch-cost>>'s lesson from the little bakery repeats here at
industrial scale: the line item you can see on an invoice (spares,
service contracts) is never the number that decides whether a fix was
worth it — the downtime bill is, and on a fourteen-station chain, it
dwarfs everything else once even a couple of stations are left
unmanaged.

[TIP]
====
Notice what did *not* change: eleven of fourteen stations, every
failure and repair distribution, the mission length, the $8 000
red-cost rate. One field (`RSO`) and one PM schedule, applied only
where the weak-link read said to apply them, is the entire difference
between these two Grand_Total figures.
====

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
Every station's Weibull clock is drawn independently — a real bottling
line often buys identical seals, motors, or bearings for several
stations from the same supplier and the same production batch, so a
bad batch can age several "independent" parts together
(<<ch-capstone>>'s common-cause caveat applies here too). The $8 000
down-hour rate and the specific spare and PM costs are teaching
numbers chosen to make the arithmetic land cleanly, not a real
bottling plant's invoice — trust the *ratio* this chapter demonstrates,
not the digits. And Case_Packer's 600-hour PM interval was picked
once, not tuned; <<ch-pm>>'s own playbook (try a few intervals, watch
what moves) was never run against it here.
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |Why does "how often it fails" equal "how much it hurts" on this
line, when <<ch-weaklink>>'s van needed two separate tables to tell
them apart?
|2 |ProdLine1 has *more* system failures per run than ProdLine0, yet
costs less and is more available. Explain why a rising failure count
is not automatically bad news here.
|3 |Filler and Capper share the same resupply policy but different
stock sizes and different per-event costs. If Capper's spare price
rose five-fold, would you still expect ProdLine1 to beat ProdLine0 on
Grand_Total? What would you check first?
|4 |Name one experiment from <<ch-pm>>'s own playbook you would run
before trusting Case_Packer's 600-hour interval.
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. Nothing here forgives a failure — no vote, no standby, no hierarchy
   layer absorbs anything. Every station sits on the one series chain,
   so a station's failure count *is* its stoppage count; VanFlat needed
   two tables only because its vote let the tires fail constantly
   without ever being blamed.
. A dead line cannot fail again — ProdLine0 spends long stretches
   stuck down between Capper's fourth failure and its eventual repair,
   during which the clock simply is not running for any station. Fix
   the shelf and the line spends far more of the year actually
   operating, which is exactly the condition under which a station
   *can* fail again. More failures at a lower cost each beats fewer
   failures that each cost far more.
. Not necessarily — check Capper's per-event spare cost against its
   own AveUsed first. At roughly nine replacements a year, a five-fold
   price rise adds real money to the Blocks line; whether it still
   beats the $2 million-plus SystemDownCost gap depends on how far
   that one number moves, not on whether the sparing policy itself is
   still sound.
. Run the interval at a couple of neighboring values (300 h, 900 h,
   say) and compare Grand_Total, the same before/after comparison
   <<ch-pm>> ran on CarCare0/CarCare1 — a single untested number is a
   guess wearing a schedule's clothing.
====

=== Exercise

Pick one of the eleven untouched stations. Read its `AveUsed` from
`Spares_Utilization` and decide honestly whether it belongs anywhere
near the top of the table you built above. If it does not, predict —
before touching the model — that giving it a Custom shelf and an RSO
resupply plan will cost money and buy almost nothing back. Then do it
anyway: pick a stock size, turn `RSO` on, run 200 trials, and check
Grand_Total. Were you right?

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Answers vary by which station you pick, but any station
outside the top three should show `AveUsed` under 3 per run — spend
money there and Grand_Total should barely move, since its Blocks cost
rises while SystemDownCost, already small for that station's share,
has almost nothing left to give back.
====

=== What you learned

* On a pure series chain, the frequency table and the importance table
  are the same table — `Spares_Utilization`'s `AveUsed` column finds
  your weak links without any extra machinery.
* `RSO` is the field that decides whether "has spares" means anything:
  off, a shelf is a countdown to a permanent stop; on, it is a plan.
* A cost comparison is the honest way to ask whether a fix was worth
  it. An investment that barely moves Availability can still be
  crushing the downtime bill that mattered all along — and one that
  moves nothing at all is money spent on a station that was never the
  problem.

<<ch-weaklink>> next turns what you just did by hand — squint at
`AveUsed`, guess, check the cost — into a repeatable two-table method,
on a system where frequency and importance genuinely disagree.
