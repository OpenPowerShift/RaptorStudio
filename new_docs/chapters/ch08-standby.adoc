== Cold Standby: The Bakery's Second Oven

*Time: about 25 minutes.*
*New ideas: cold standby, switch priority, the cost of waking up.*

=== You will learn

* What *cold standby* means and how it differs from parallel running.
* How the engine picks which unit runs and which sleeps.
* Why a switch takes time, and what that costs you.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-sparing>> finished.
* The file `models/Bakery1.rbds`.
+
opsmodel:Bakery1[]

=== The story

A bakery cannot sell bread without one working oven. Running *two*
ovens side by side would work — that is <<ch-redundancy>>. But two
full ovens burning all day is expensive, and both wear out twice as
collectively fast.

Instead: **one oven bakes, one oven waits in the cold**. The standby
oven does not age while it waits. When the main oven fails, the baker
switches over. Waking a cold oven takes about half an hour — you
cannot bake during that gap.

Mission: one **720-hour** month. How often is the bakery dark?

=== One new idea: the cold node

<<ch-redundancy>>'s OR node kept *both* pumps running. A **cold standby node**
keeps exactly one input running and holds the rest asleep:

image::bakery-loaded.png[Bakery1: two ovens feeding a cold-swap node]

Open `models/Bakery1.rbds`. The pieces:

[cols="3,6",options="header"]
|===
|Piece |Setting
|Main_Oven |Exponential failures, mean 300 h; Lognormal repair ≈ 6 h
|Standby_Oven |same dice as Main_Oven
|Ovens_cold_swap (node) |need 1 of 2, **Cold** on, one active slot
|Link priorities |Main_Oven priority 25, Standby_Oven priority 50 — lower number wins the active slot
|Switch time |automatic switch after 0.5 h
|Power |series block behind the ovens, as always
|===

[TIP]
====
Read "cold" literally: a sleeping machine collects no operating hours,
so its failure clock stands still. That is the whole economic argument
for standby over hot redundancy.
====

=== Run it and read the story

Press F5. Then open the *Event Log* for trial 1. It opens like this:

----
0.000000   Block  Standby_Oven   Standby   Too_many_on
180.974    Block  Main_Oven      Failed    Operating_time=180.974304
180.974    Node   Ovens_cold_swap  Bad     0/2 ...
181.474    Block  Standby_Oven   Good      Prior=Standby
185.791    Block  Main_Oven      Repaired
----

Four beats, worth naming:

. **Kickoff (hour 0).** Both ovens start running; the node notices
   only one may, and puts Standby_Oven to sleep (`Too_many_on`).
. **Failure.** Main_Oven dies at hour 181. The node has zero live
   inputs — the bakery goes red.
. **The wake-up gap.** Half an hour later the standby wakes. Note its
   log line says `Prior=Standby`: it slept, so it aged zero hours.
. **Repair race.** The main oven gets fixed ~5 h later and returns.
   Now the node has two candidates again — it keeps the running one
   and sends the repaired oven to the shelf.

Typical numbers at 20 trials: availability about **0.997**, about 3
stoppage events per month.

[NOTE]
====
Compare with a hot-redundant pair of the same ovens (<<ch-redundancy>> style):
no wake-up gap ever, but both ovens burn hours from minute zero. Cold
standby trades a short dark gap at every switchover for a much longer
total service life. Which wins depends on how much a dark half hour
costs your bakery.
====

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
The two ovens fail independently, and the switch itself never fails —
this chapter's Cold node always succeeds at waking the standby unit
within its 0.5 h. Real kitchens have shared risks a cold-standby
diagram does not draw: a grease fire or a breaker trip can take out
both ovens at once, and the transfer switch itself is a physical part
that can jam. RAPTOR Studio can model an imperfect switch — a chance
the transfer itself fails, not covered in this book — to relax the
second assumption; shared physical
risk to both units needs dependency modelling to relax the first.
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |While Standby_Oven sleeps, what happens to its failure clock?
|2 |Why did the system flash red between the failure (hour 181.0) and
the wake-up (hour 181.5)?
|3 |If both ovens had priority 25, which would run first?
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. Nothing — a sleeping unit collects no operating hours, so its
   failure clock is frozen until it wakes.
. The node had zero live inputs during the switch: the standby could
   not take over instantly. Those 0.5 hours are real dark time for
   the bakery, visible as a red blip in the timeline.
. Tie on priority number; the engine keeps whichever input reached it
   first in link order — here Main_Oven, since its link was defined
   first. Ties are legal but sloppy; always give distinct priorities.
====

=== Exercise

Baker's nightmare: make the switch *manual* instead of automatic. In
each oven→node link set AProb from `1` to `0.9`, AutoTime to `0`,
ManTime to `3`. Now 10% of switches need the owner to drive in:
3 extra dark hours each time. Run 50 trials before and after, and
compare availability.

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Availability drops noticeably — typically from ~0.997 to
~0.98–0.985 at 50 trials. Each switchover now risks a 3-hour manual
drive-in, and with several failures per month those extra hours pile
up fast.
====

=== What you learned

* A cold node keeps k = 1 units running and parks the rest.
* Link priority numbers decide who runs; ties break by order.
* Sleeping units do not age; waking them costs a visible gap.

schedule.
