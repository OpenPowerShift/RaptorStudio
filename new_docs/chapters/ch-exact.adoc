== Exact Answers: Checking a Simulation with the Analytical Solver

*Time: about 30 minutes.*
*New ideas: the exact (analytical) solver, checking a simulation against
it, steady-state availability, what the solver cannot do.*

=== You will learn

* How to get an exact answer for a simple model, with no trials and no
  noise.
* How to check a simulation against the exact answer, with the SEM from
  <<ch-trials>>.
* What a difference between the two tells you.
* What the solver cannot model, and when only simulation will do.

=== You need

* <<ch-trials>> finished. You use the SEM (standard error of the mean)
  from that chapter.
* `models/Lantern1.rbds`, `models/Vendo1.rbds` and
  `models/WaterTreatment.rbds`.
+
opsmodel:Lantern1[]

=== The story

In <<ch-trials>> you ran the lantern 1000 times. The answer settled
"near 0.38", and the chapter called that the long-run truth. But how do
you know the truth is 0.38? Every simulation result is a measurement,
and a measurement has noise.

For a simple model, you do not have to measure. You can *calculate*.
RAPTOR Studio has a second tool for this, the **analytical solver**. It
works out the answer from the diagram and the failure distributions
directly. There are no trials, so there is no noise.

That gives you a reference. If the simulation and the exact answer
agree, you can trust the model and the simulation more. If they do not
agree, something is different, and you can find out what.

=== One new idea: measure or calculate

Think of a coin. To find the chance of heads, you can toss it 1000
times and count. That is a simulation. Or you can say "two sides, equal
chance, so 0.5". That is a calculation.

The calculation is exact, but only when its assumptions are true. If
the coin is bent, the calculation is wrong and the tosses are right.

The solver and the simulation work the same way:

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
| |Simulation |Analytical solver
|How |Runs the system many times with random failures |Calculates from the diagram and the distributions
|Answer |An estimate, with an SEM |Exact, no SEM
|Models |Everything: repairs, PM, spares, crews, standby, phases |A simpler subset (see <<exact-limits>>)
|Speed |Seconds to hours |Usually under a second; minutes for a very large model
|===

Use each one to check the other.

=== Your first exact answer

. Open `Lantern1.rbds`.
. Click *Model ▸ Analytical…*. The solver window opens. It does not
  solve yet.
. Look at the settings. *Mode* is *Reliability — no repairs, R(t)*.
  *Mission time* is 48 hours, the model's run length.
. Click *Compute*.

image::exact-summary.png[The solver window for Lantern1: reliability at 48 hours 0.384604, MTTF 43.1426 hours, B10 life 9.15389 hours]

The *Summary* tab shows one card for each result:

* *Reliability at 48 hours: 0.384604.* The chance that a lantern works
  for the whole 48-hour trip. <<ch-trials>> measured "near 0.38". Now
  you have the exact value.
* *MTTF: 43.1426 hours.* The mean time to the first failure of the
  lantern. A 48-hour simulation cannot measure this directly. It stops
  at 48 hours, so it never sees the lanterns that fail later.
* *B10 life: 9.15389 hours.* By this time, 10% of lanterns have failed.
* *Hazard rate* and *Failure density* describe how fast failures happen
  at 48 hours. You do not need them in this chapter.

The window never changes your model. You can try settings freely.

=== Check the solver by hand

The solver is not a black box. For a series system you can check it
with a calculator.

The lantern is three blocks in series: Battery_Pack, Switch and
LED_Panel. The lantern works only if all three work. So the system
reliability is the product of the three block reliabilities. Open the
*Elements* tab to see each one:

[cols="3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Block |Reliability at 48 h
|Battery_Pack (Weibull, shape 1.5, scale 60 h) |0.4889
|Switch (Exponential, mean 200 h) |0.7866
|LED_Panel (Normal, mean 100 h, SD 10 h) |0.9999999
|*Lantern (product)* |*0.4889 × 0.7866 × 1.0 = 0.3846*
|===

image::exact-elements.png[The Elements tab for Lantern1: each block's reliability and MTTF, with the Birnbaum, RAW, RRW, Fussell–Vesely and criticality importance measures]

The product is the solver's answer. For parallel, k-out-of-n and
bridge diagrams the algebra is longer, but the solver does it exactly
in the same way.

The *Elements* tab also shows **importance measures**. They tell you
which block matters most. The simplest is *Birnbaum*: how much the
system reliability changes when this block goes from failed to perfect.
For a series system, the Birnbaum value of a block is the product of
the *other* blocks' reliabilities:

* Battery_Pack: 0.7866 × 1.0 = 0.7866.
* Switch: 0.4889 × 1.0 = 0.4889.

*Criticality* sorts the table. Battery_Pack is first, with 0.65: it
causes about two thirds of the lantern failures. LED_Panel is last.
It almost never fails within 48 hours. If you want a better lantern,
improve the battery first.

TIP: Hover over a column heading to read what the measure means. Hover
over a number to see all its digits.

=== Use the exact answer to check a simulation

Now check the simulation from <<ch-trials>>.

. Close the solver window.
. Set *Trials* to 2000 and click *Run*.
. Read *Reliability (Mean)* and its SEM in the Results panel.

A run of 2000 trials gave 0.3775 with an SEM of 0.0108. Your numbers
can be slightly different. Compare the simulation with the exact value:

----
difference = 0.3846 - 0.3775 = 0.0071
in SEMs    = 0.0071 / 0.0108 = 0.66
----

Use this rule:

[cols="2,5",options="header"]
|===
|Difference |What it means
|Up to 2 × SEM |They agree. This is normal noise.
|2 to 3 × SEM |Probably noise. Run more trials and look again.
|More than 3 × SEM |A real difference. Find out why.
|===

Here the difference is 0.66 SEM, so the two agree. The model and the
simulation give the same answer, and the solver confirms the number in
<<ch-trials>>.

Why does the rule use 2 × SEM? About 95% of simulations land within
2 × SEM of the truth. So a difference of less than 2 × SEM is the
noise you expect. A difference of more than 3 × SEM happens by chance
in fewer than 3 runs in 1000.

=== Availability: the long-run fraction of time up

Reliability mode assumes no repairs: one failure and the system is
finished. For a repairable system, use availability mode.

. Open `Vendo1.rbds`, the vending machine from <<ch-repair>>.
. Click *Model ▸ Analytical…*.
. Set *Mode* to *Availability — steady state*.
. Click *Compute*.

The result is *Availability 0.970685*. Again you can check it by hand.
For one block with repair, the long-run availability is

----
A = MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR)
----

[cols="3,2,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Block |MTTF |MTTR |Availability
|Keypad |600 h |12 h |600 / 612 = 0.98039
|Compressor |400 h |4 h |400 / 404 = 0.99010
|*Machine (series, product)* | | |*0.98039 × 0.99010 = 0.97069*
|===

A simulation of 200 trials gave 0.97098 with an SEM of 0.00041. The
difference is 0.00029, which is 0.7 SEM. They agree.

NOTE: *Steady state* means "after a long time". The solver gives the
fraction of time up when the system has run for a very long time. A
simulation of one year includes the start, when every part is new.
When the run is short compared with the MTTF of the parts, the two
answers are different, and both are correct. They answer different
questions.

=== When they disagree: what the gap tells you

Open `WaterTreatment.rbds` and the solver. Click *Compute*.

image::exact-refused.png[The solver refuses WaterTreatment: block 1 Intake_Screen has a repair distribution, but reliability mode means non-repairable]

The solver refuses the model. It names the first block it cannot handle
and says why. This plant has repairs, preventive maintenance (PM) on its
six pumps, and a small pool of spare motors: 2 in stock, a new one every
4380 hours (six months), or an emergency order in 96 hours. The solver
cannot model PM or spares.

You can tell the solver to leave these things out. It then gives the
answer for a simpler plant:

. Set *Mode* to *Availability — steady state*.
. Tick *Ignore ▸ Preventive maintenance*, *Spares* and *Resources*.
. Click *Compute*.

The solver gives 0.99785. The *Assumptions* tab lists what it left
out. This is the availability of the same plant with no PM and a spare
motor always on the shelf.

A simulation of the real plant (1000 trials) gave 0.99425, with an SEM
of 0.00005. The difference is 0.0036, which is about 70 SEM. That is a
real difference, not noise. It is 0.36% of the time, or about 31 hours
of downtime a year.

What causes the gap? The solver cannot tell you, but simulation can.
Simulate copies of the plant, each with one thing removed:

[cols="5,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Copy of the plant |Simulated availability |Difference from 0.99785
|As built |0.99425 |+0.0036 (70 SEM)
|No PM |0.99791 |-0.00006 (1.4 SEM): agrees
|PM, but unlimited spare motors |0.99577 |+0.0021 (46 SEM)
|No PM, unlimited spare motors |0.99789 |-0.00004 (1.0 SEM): agrees
|No PM, unlimited spares, 10-year run |0.99787 |-0.00002 (1.3 SEM): agrees
|===

The table tells the story:

* *PM causes the whole gap.* With PM switched off, the simulation agrees
  with the exact answer.
* *Part of the PM cost is spares.* Each PM uses a spare motor. With only
  2 in stock, a pump that fails after a PM often waits for a motor.
  Unlimited motors recover about 40% of the gap. The rest is the PM
  downtime itself.
* *Spares alone do not matter.* With PM off, unlimited motors change
  nothing.
* *The start of the run does not matter here.* A 10-year run gives the
  same answer as a 1-year run, so the new-plant start-up is not part of
  the gap.

So the fix is to look at the PM plan, or to stock more motors, not to
buy better pumps.

NOTE: PM does not always lower availability. For parts that wear out,
PM can raise it, because it replaces a part before it fails. The solver
cannot tell you which way PM goes. Simulation can.

When you see a real difference, do this:

. Read the *Assumptions* tab. List everything the solver left out or
  treated differently.
. Ask: "Do I expect these things to change the answer?" Waiting for
  spares or crews lowers availability. PM, standby and phases can move
  it either way.
. If you expect *no* difference, look for a mistake in the model. Check
  the distributions and their units, the links, and the k-out-of-n
  values.
. To find the cause, simulate copies of the model with one feature
  removed at a time, as in the table above. When the simulation agrees
  with the exact answer, you have found what causes the gap.

=== What the solver cannot do

The solver gives exact answers only for a subset of models. It refuses
a model it cannot solve, and it names the first block or node that
stops it. It never gives an approximate answer without telling you.

[cols="3,4,4",options="header"]
|===
|Feature |What the solver does |What to do
|Repairs, reliability mode |Refuses. Reliability mode means no repairs. |Tick *Ignore ▸ Repairs* for the reliability up to the first failure, or use availability mode.
|Preventive maintenance, spares, crews |Refuses. |Tick *Ignore* for the answer with perfect support. Simulate for the real answer.
|Cold standby |Refuses until you choose. |Choose *Standby*: treat the standby paths as always in service, or as absent. *Absent* is a lower limit. *Always in service* is not an upper limit: a cold spare does not age while it waits, so the real system can be better. Switching delays and switch failures make it worse. Simulate for the real answer.
|Phases |Refuses until you choose. |Choose one *Phase*: the solver assumes the system stays in that phase all the time. Or choose *All phases, one at a time*: it solves each phase on its own, and *Showing phase* above the results picks one to see. It does not model the changes from phase to phase.
|Dependencies |Refuses until you choose. |Choose *Dependencies*: up (ignore the dependency) or down (remove the dependent parts). See <<ch-dependency>>.
|Capacity and flow |Ignores them, with a warning. |Success means "a path from Start to End works". Simulate for flow.
|Events other than a fixed success probability |Refuses. |Simulate.
|Start-up behaviour |Availability mode gives the steady state only. |Simulate for a short mission.
|===

The most important limit is **independence**. The solver assumes that
every part fails on its own. A real system can have a *common cause*:
one power feed, one flood, one bad batch of parts. If two parallel pumps
share a power feed, they can fail together. The solver does not see
this. For parallel parts, its answer is then *too optimistic*. The
simulation has the same limit unless you model the common cause, for
example with a dependency or a shared block.

=== A checklist for checking a simulation

Use this list when you build a new model, or when a result surprises
you.

. Make a simple version of the model that the solver accepts. Often you
  only tick *Ignore* boxes. Sometimes you solve one hierarchy at a time
  (see <<exact-large>>).
. Get the exact answer.
. Simulate the same simple version with enough trials to make the SEM
  small.
. Compare them with the 2 × SEM rule. They must agree. If they do not
  agree, the model has a mistake, or the simulation does something the
  solver does not.
. Simulate the full model. Explain the gap from the exact answer with
  the features the solver left out.
. Read the importance measures. The most critical parts must make
  engineering sense. A surprise in the importance table often shows a
  wrong distribution or a wrong link.

[[exact-large]]
=== Large models

The solver builds a *decision diagram* of the model. For most models
this takes less than a second. When the paths through a model share
many blocks, the diagram can grow very large.

* The progress bar beside *Compute* shows one percentage for the whole
  solve, with the stage and the time left.
* *Cancel*, or closing the window, stops the solve.
* If the diagram grows past its limit, the solver stops and tells you.
  It offers a larger limit, and shows how much memory that needs.
* For a very large model, choose one *Hierarchy* in the settings and
  solve it on its own. The solver treats the hierarchy's input as always
  available.

=== Keep and export the results

The window keeps every result you calculate while it is open. If you
change a setting and then change it back, the earlier result shows
again at once.

*Export ▸ This result…* saves the result as a spreadsheet. *Export ▸
Reliability and availability…* saves both modes with the same settings
in one workbook. If you have not calculated the other mode, the solver
calculates it first. The page still shows your result. The file name
tells you what is in it, for example
`Vendo1_analytical_reliability_availability.xlsx`.

The same solver is on the command line and in the AI assistant
(<<ch-mcp>>):

----
raptor analytic models/Vendo1.rbds --mode both --xlsx .
----

  answer is 0.3846. Do they agree?

  mode?

  0.99426 ± 0.00011. Which answer is correct?

  has a reliability of 0.99. Is the real value higher or lower?

.Answers

  2 SEM, so it is normal noise.

  repairs", so the solver will not pretend that the repairs do not
  exist. Tick *Ignore ▸ Repairs* to get the reliability up to the first

=== What you learned

* The analytical solver calculates an exact answer from the diagram,
  with no trials and no noise.
* For simple diagrams you can check it by hand: a product of block
  reliabilities in series, `MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR)` for one repaired block.
* A simulation agrees with the exact answer when the difference is less
  than 2 × SEM. More than 3 × SEM is a real difference.
* A real difference is the effect of what the solver left out, or a
  mistake in the model. The *Assumptions* tab lists what was left out.
  Simulate copies with one feature removed at a time to find which.
* The solver does not model PM, spares, crews, flow or common causes. It
  assumes every part fails independently. Use simulation for those, and
  use the solver to check the parts it can do.
