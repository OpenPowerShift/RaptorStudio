[[ch-voting]]
== Voting: A Drone That Survives Losing Motors

*Time: about 20 minutes.*
*New ideas: the k-of-n vote node, mixing series and parallel structure,
degraded-but-working states.*

=== You will learn

* How a *vote* node works: "need k of n".
* How to combine a series part with a voted group.
* Why the system health bar shows yellow.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> and <<ch-redundancy>> finished.
* The file `models/Drone1.rbds`.
+
opsmodel:Drone1[]

=== The story

A delivery drone has six motors. Here is the fun part of aircraft
design: *it does not need all six.* Four healthy motors can hold the
frame steady. Five is comfortable. Three is a crash.

So the drone flies while **at least 4 of its 6 motors** work. This
rule is called a **k-of-n vote**. Your laptop's battery cells, a
data centre's servers, a rocket's engines — same trick.

The rest of the drone stays simple:

* one **Battery** — no spare, no flying without it,
* one **Flight_Controller** — its brain.

Mission: fly **48 hours** of total service time. What are the odds?

=== One new idea: the vote node

image::kofn-drone.svg[Six motors feeding a need-4-of-6 node]

The model has three layers:

. **Series start:** Battery sits alone in the flow.
. **Voted middle:** all six motors feed one node with rule *need 4
   of 6*.
. **Series end:** Flight_Controller, then End.

[TIP]
====
Series, redundancy and voting are really one idea wearing three
costumes. "Need n
of n" is series. "Need 1 of n" is parallel redundancy. Any k in
between is a vote. One knob — the k — gives you every flavor.
====

=== Open the model

Open `models/Drone1.rbds` and select the **Motors_need_4** node. In the
Inspector you see k = 4, n = 6. Change those two numbers here when you
do the exercise.

The block data:

[cols="3,2,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Block |Failure dist |Param 1 |Param 2
|Battery |Normal |60 |6
|Motor_1 … Motor_6 |Exponential |120 |—
|Flight_Controller |Weibull |1.2 |300
|===

Mission settings: stop at **48 hours**, 10 trials.

=== Run it

Press F5. With many trials, long-run reliability settles near **0.59**
— call it a coin flip with good odds. Now look at the Results
*Overview* tab and find the **System health** bar:

image::drone-loaded.png[Drone1 on the canvas]

You will often see a **yellow** slice. Yellow means *degraded*: the
drone lost a motor or two, but still flies. The engine tracks three
colours at every instant:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
|Green |every block that matters is up
|Yellow |some blocks down, but the system still works
|Red |system down
|===

Redundancy does not just raise reliability — it buys you *warning
time*. The yellow hours between "first motor died" and "third motor
died" are your landing window.

=== Do the math check (optional but satisfying)

Each motor survives 48 hours with probability e^−48/120 ≈ 0.67. The
chance that at least four of six survive works out to about 0.69. The
battery and controller survive with about 0.98 and 0.90. Multiply:
0.69 × 0.98 × 0.90 ≈ 0.61 — right where the simulator lands (0.59).

When a simulation agrees with pencil-and-paper theory, both deserve
trust. For big models, only the simulator survives.

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
A k-of-n vote only protects you against *independent* motor failures.
Six real drone motors share one battery, one frame, and often one
electronic speed controller board — a hard landing or a battery fault
can take out several at once, in a single shared instant no k-of-n
vote can absorb. The 0.59 reliability figure is the best case: motors
failing for unrelated reasons, one at a time. A real flight-safety case
would need to add that shared risk explicitly, not trust the vote node
alone.
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |What k would turn Motors_need_4 into a pure series chain?
|2 |What k would make it a full 6-deep redundancy?
|3 |A trial ends with reliability counted how — green, not-yellow,
or something else?
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. k = 4, n = 4 — every motor required.
. k = 1 — any single motor keeps the drone airborne (physically
   silly, mathematically fine).
. Reliability counts trials that finish with the system never going
   red. Yellow time inside a trial still counts as a success.
====

=== Exercise

Cheap airline: the operator removes Motor_6 to save weight and cost.
Set k back to 4 but delete Motor_5 and Motor_6 so only 4 motors feed
the node (n = 4). Predict reliability before running. Then run 100
trials. Was losing two motors worth it?

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Reliability falls hard — roughly 0.42 at 100 trials. Losing
two of six motors removes most of the vote's protection: the node now
fails on the second motor loss.
====

=== What you learned

* A k-of-n node votes: system up while k of n inputs live.
* Series = need all, parallel = need one, vote = anything between.
* Degraded (yellow) time is real, visible, and useful.
