[[ch-cost]]
== Cost: The Bill Arrives

*Time: about 30 minutes.*
*New ideas: cost analysis, purchase vs. operating cost, the price of
lost flow, reading the COST report.*

=== You will learn

* What the engine bills for: repairs, downtime, and (with <<ch-capacity>>)
  every lost megalitre.
* How the COST section is organised — Operating, Life_cycle_cost,
  Cost_Summary, Grand_Total.
* Why the biggest line item is usually one you never budgeted.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-capacity>> finished.
* The files `models/Water2.rbds` (the water utility, now with a bill).
+
opsmodel:Water2[]

=== The story

The pumps held, the pipes held — and then the invoice from reality
arrived. Every pump repair sends a crew (money per hour) and a
callout fee. Every hour the town is completely dry, the utility
trucks in emergency water. And every megalitre that never made it
through the pipes — the yellow-time shortfall of <<ch-capacity>> — has a
price too.

Simulation already knew about all of it. Cost analysis just adds up
the arithmetic.

=== Three places money lives

Open `models/Water2.rbds` and choose **Model → Project Settings**:

image::water2-sysconfig.png[Project Settings: cost fields for the water utility]

Money is configured in three places:

* **Per block** — each pump carries its own price tag: 40 000 to buy,
  a 2 000 callout per repair, and 100 per repair-hour of crew time.
  (These live on the block's row in the `.rbds` file — fourteen cost
  fields covering purchase, running, repair, PM, and spares.)
* **System red cost: 5 000 per hour** — the emergency-trucking bill
  while the whole town is dry.
* **Cost of lost flow: 40 per megalitre** — <<ch-capacity>>'s shortfall,
  now with a price.

[NOTE]
====
Cost analysis and capacity analysis are independent switches. With
cost on but capacity off, you still get repair and downtime bills —
there is simply no lost-flow line, because nothing is being lost.
====

=== Run it

Run **200 trials** of the 8760-hour mission. The end-of-run report
grows a ***COST*** section. For one typical run:

[cols="4,2",options="header"]
|===
|Cost section row |This run
|Operating → Repair totals |about 180 000
|Life_cycle_cost → Purchase |120 000
|Cost_Summary → SystemLostFlowCost |about 2 200 000
|Cost_Summary → SystemDownCost |0.00
|Grand_Total |about 2 400 000
|===

Averaged over the year of 200 trials:

[cols="4,2",options="header"]
|===
|Where the money goes |Per year
|Lost water (40 × shortfall) |about 2 500 000
|Pump repairs (callout + crew) |about 215 000
|Pumps themselves (bought once) |120 000
|*Grand total* |*about 2 900 000*
|===

Read that table twice. The three pumps — bought, crewed, called out —
cost about 335 000 a year. The *water the town never received* costs
2 500 000. The failure bill is not the repair bill; it is the
shortfall bill, and it is seven times larger.

[TIP]
====
SystemDownCost is zero here because the town is almost never fully
dry (<<ch-capacity>>: red about 0.007% of the year). If your cost report
shows a fat SystemDownCost row, your problem is stoppages; if it
shows a fat SystemLostFlowCost row, your problem is *size* — and no
repair crew on earth can fix size.
====

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
Every price in this model is a flat, unchanging number: a repair costs
the same in year one and year ten, and a dollar today is worth exactly
one dollar next year. Real budgeting cares about *when* money moves —
a dollar spent buying reliability today is worth more than a dollar
saved on downtime a decade out, once you discount for inflation and
the time value of money. This engine happily totals costs across any
mission length you set, but it will not discount them for you; a
multi-year business case built on these numbers needs that layer added
by hand afterward.
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |Why does the Repair column differ from the callout fee times the
number of failures?
|2 |The utility doubles the price of water to 80 per megalitre. Which
report rows change, and which stay put?
|3 |Purchase cost appears once in Life_cycle_cost but the mission is
one year. What happens to its share of Grand_Total on a ten-year study?
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. The Repair column bills the *whole visit*: the 2 000 callout plus
   the crew's 100 per hour for a 24-hour fix — 4 400 per failure,
   times roughly sixteen failures per pump per year.
. Only SystemLostFlowCost doubles (and Grand_Total follows). Repair,
   Purchase, and SystemDownCost stay put: the physics did not change,
   only the price tag attached to the shortfall.
. Purchase is charged once at the start of each run, so it stays
   120 000 while ten years of repair and shortfall bills stack up
   around it. Its *share* of Grand_Total shrinks — one reason long
   studies make durable equipment look good.
====

=== Exercise

<<ch-capacity>>'s exercise upsized Pump_C's pipe to nominal 60 / max 100.
Do it again on `Water2.rbds` (edit that link's `nomFlow`/`MaxFlow` in
the file), run 100 trials, and compare grand totals. Estimate the
yearly saving, then answer: if the bigger pipe costs 250 000 to
install, how soon does it pay for itself?

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
The upsize cuts the shortfall from about 63 000 to about
50 000 megalitres a year, so the lost-water bill drops from about
2.5 M to about 2.0 M — a saving near 500 000 per year. A 250 000
pipe pays for itself in about six months. (Your trial-to-trial
numbers will wander; the saving is far larger than the noise.)
====

=== What you learned

* Cost analysis sums three streams: per-block costs, red-hour costs,
  and lost-flow costs.
* The COST section reads top to bottom: per-block Operating, then
  Life_cycle_cost, then Cost_Summary and Grand_Total.
* The dominant cost is often the invisible one — flow that never
  arrived.
