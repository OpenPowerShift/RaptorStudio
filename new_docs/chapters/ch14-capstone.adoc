== Capstone: The Data Centre, Engineered to a Target

*Time: about 45 minutes.*
*New ideas: availability targets, N / N+1 / 2N design vocabulary,
tuning a system against a standard, and every tool from the earlier
chapters working together.*

=== You will learn

* How real availability tiers are stated — and what a "five nines"
  demand actually means in minutes per year.
* How redundancy vocabulary (N, N+1, 2N) maps onto the RBD pieces you
  have been drawing since <<ch-voting>>.
* The tuning loop: measure, find the weak link, fix it, re-measure —
  until the number is met.

=== You need

* <<ch-first-model>> through <<ch-weaklink>> finished — this chapter uses all of them.
* The files `models/DC1.rbds` (baseline) and `models/DC2.rbds` (tuned).

=== The story

A colocation provider wants a new hall and hands you a slide with one
number on it: **99.98% availability**. That is the neighbourhood of
what the data-centre industry calls a *Tier III* facility — Uptime
Institute's tier scheme runs from about 99.67% (Tier I) to about
99.995% (Tier IV), and TIA-942 uses a similar rated-1-to-4 scale.
Put the target in hours: one year is 8 760 hours, so 99.98% allows
about **1.6 hours of downtime per year**. Everything else in this
chapter is the pursuit of those 96 minutes.

The industry's shorthand for redundancy:

* **N** — just enough capacity for the load. One of it fails, you're
  down.
* **N+1** — one spare beyond need (<<ch-voting>>'s k-of-n vote, one
  forgiven failure).
* **2N** — a complete second copy of everything (two full chains,
  either alone carries the load).

You own the tools for all three. Time to build.

=== The facility, drawn as three ovals

Open `models/DC1.rbds`. <<ch-hierarchy>>'s hierarchy idea, at building
scale — the home level is just three ovals in series (power, cooling,
IT), because the hall works only if *all three* work:

image::dc-loaded.png[DC1 home level: Power_sub, Cooling_sub, IT_sub in series]

Step inside each oval (double-click, as in <<ch-hierarchy>>):

* **Power_sub** — the grid feed `Utility_A` with a cold-standby
  `Generator` behind the automatic transfer switch (<<ch-standby>>'s
  pattern: the ATS needs 0.1 h to wake the genset), then `UPS`, then
  `Switchgear`.
* **Cooling_sub** — three `CRAC` units voting 2-of-3 (N+1 cooling),
  feeding a `Chill_Pump`. All three CRACs draw replacement parts from
  one shared shelf, `CRAC_Spares` (<<ch-sparing>>'s Pooled type, not
  Custom): three units in stock, resupplied every 30 days, with
  emergency shipping if the shelf ever actually runs dry. One shared
  shelf instead of three private ones is the realistic choice here —
  a facility rarely stocks a private spare per unit when the units are
  interchangeable.
* **IT_sub** — dual `PDU`s (either carries), then three `Server_Row`s
  voting 2-of-3 (N+1 rows).

image::dc-power-inside.png[Inside Power_sub: utility, standby generator, ATS, UPS, switchgear]

Twelve blocks, three hierarchies, one cold standby pair, two votes,
one series backbone — every shape from the first ten chapters of this
book, wearing hard hats.

=== Run the baseline

Run **200 trials** of the 8 760-hour mission:

[cols="4,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |DC1 result
|Availability |about 99.948%
|Downtime per year |about 4.5 h
|MTBDE |about 1 090 h
|MDT |about 0.5 h
|Stoppages per year |about 9
|===

Ninety-nine point nine five percent sounds excellent — and misses the
target by nearly three times. The downtime budget was 1.6 hours; the
facility spends 4.5. Now do what <<ch-weaklink>> taught: *blame someone*.
Read the event logs and ask which part was down at each red:

[cols="3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Blamed for downtime |Hours per year
|ATS transfer gaps (utility out) |about 0.8
|Switchgear (single string, N) |about 2.1
|UPS |about 0.6
|Generator fails while carrying |about 0.5
|Chill_Pump |about 0.4
|===

The table reads like a to-do list, sorted.

=== Tune it: three moves

Open `models/DC2.rbds` — the same facility after the design review.
Three changes, each answering one line of the blame table:

1. **Dual switchgear strings, one under PM.** `Switchgear_A` and
   `Switchgear_B` now sit in parallel — 2N for the distribution
   layer. Weekly preventive maintenance (<<ch-pm>>) on string A is
   now *concurrent*: string B carries the load for those two hours,
   so service costs zero availability. This is exactly what the
   standards mean by *concurrently maintainable*.
2. **A faster transfer switch.** ATS gap 0.1 h → 0.05 h. The utility
   still fails eight-odd times a year; each transfer now costs half
   as much.
3. **A priority spares contract.** UPS and pump repairs drop from
   4 h to 2 h (edit their repair parameter). Same failures, half the
   outage each.

Note what the moves have in common: none of them makes any part
*less likely to fail*. They shrink the *consequence* of failure —
<<ch-weaklink>>'s other dial.

=== Run the tuned facility

Run **200 trials** again:

[cols="4,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Measure |DC1 |DC2
|Availability |about 99.948% |about *99.984%*
|Downtime per year |about 4.5 h |about *1.4 h*
|MDT |about 0.50 h |about 0.15 h
|Yellow time (degraded) |about 1.6% |about 2.8%
|===

The target was 1.6 hours; DC2 comes in at about 1.4 — Tier III
reached, with a little margin. Notice the price, though: yellow time
nearly doubled. Redundancy converts hard stoppages into soft
degradation — the CRAC that used to stop the hall now merely trims
its margin, the PM that used to be an outage is now a yawn on the
health bar. A well-tuned facility is *yellower* than a fragile one.

[NOTE]
====
The simulator does not certify anything. Real standards compliance
depends on construction, commissioning, operations, and audit —
fault-tree detail no 12-block model carries. What the model *does*
buy you is the ranking: which design moves buy hours, and which buy
nothing. Spend the model, then spend the concrete.
====

And <<ch-cost>>'s question — what does downtime cost? — has a blunt
answer here. At a data-centre rate of 300 000 per down-hour, DC1's
year costs about **1.36 million** in outages; DC2's about **0.42
million**. The redundancy was not free to build, but the model says
it pays for itself in months.

.Limits of this model
[NOTE]
====
Two honesty checks belong on every number in this chapter. First,
independence: every failure in DC1/DC2 is drawn on its own private
dice, exactly like every earlier chapter — but a real hall has shared
risk a twelve-block diagram cannot see (one utility substation feeding
both "independent" strings, one cooling loop, one fire-suppression
system). This is **common-cause failure**, and it is the single
biggest reason paper redundancy calculations disappoint in practice —
model it explicitly with dependency, or treat every redundancy number
here as an optimistic ceiling. Second, precision: before quoting
99.984% to a customer, do the arithmetic <<ch-trials>> taught you,
rather than trusting a headline number on faith.
====

=== Is 99.984% a real number, or 200-trial noise?

DC2's own 200-trial run reports Availability Mean 0.999835, SEM
0.0000140. Apply <<ch-trials>>'s own rule of thumb:

> *Answer ≈ Mean ± 2 × SEM = 0.999835 ± 0.0000280, roughly
> 0.999807 to 0.999863.*

The whole interval sits above the 99.98% (0.9998) target — DC2 clears
its Tier III number with real margin, not by luck of the draw. That
might be surprising: the
xref:../docs/formulas.html#rare-events[Formulas quick reference]
warned that pinning down a rare event can need tens of thousands of
trials. The difference is *what* is being counted. A four-nines
**Reliability** figure (ch-events' own territory) is one 0-or-1 coin
flip per run — you need enormous trial counts to see enough of the
rare "1" outcomes to trust the average. **Availability** is not a coin
flip: every one of these 200 runs already measures a full 8 760-hour
year, hour by hour, so even one run carries far more information about
the system's true up/down ratio than one Reliability trial ever could.
Precision comes cheap here because the metric itself is generous with
data, not because 200 is a magic number — a model reporting Reliability
at these odds would need <<ch-trials>>'s rare-event math in earnest.

[NOTE]
====
The normal-approximation interval above is least trustworthy exactly
at the extremes (fractions very near 0 or 1) — the same warning
<<ch-trials>> gives for Reliability. It happens to hold up fine here
because 2×SEM (0.0000280) is tiny next to the distance from 1.0 (about
0.00016): the interval never gets close enough to the boundary to
distort. Always check that distance before trusting the interval, not
just the SEM on its own.
====

=== Check yourself

[cols="1,4"]
|===
|1 |The three server rows vote 2-of-3. In N/N+1 vocabulary, what is
that, and what failure does it *not* protect against?
|2 |DC2 doubled the switchgear but kept a single utility feed and a
single generator. Name one failure that still stops the whole hall.
|3 |Why did yellow time *rise* after tuning, and why is that the
expected signature of a good design?
|4 |All three CRACs draw from one shared spare shelf instead of each
keeping its own. Name one advantage and one risk of sharing.
|===

.Answers
[%collapsible]
====
. N+1 — one row beyond the two needed. It forgives any *single*
   row failure, but not two at once, and not a failure of the PDU
   pair or anything upstream of the vote.
. Plenty of candidates: the generator failing while it carries the
   load during a utility outage, the UPS, the Chill_Pump, or both
   PDUs failing together — each is still a single point that stops
   every string. 2N switchgear removed only the switchgear's own
   entry from the blame table.
. Redundancy converts stoppages into degradations: a failed CRAC or
   row now trims margin (yellow) instead of stopping the hall (red),
   and PM happens under cover of the spare string. Yellow rising
   while red falls is the health-bar signature of consequence being
   traded for uptime.
. Advantage: one shelf of 3 covers whatever mix of CRACs actually
   fails — three private shelves of one spare each would need to
   restock the *specific* unit that broke, wasting capacity sitting
   idle on the other two shelves. Risk: two CRACs failing close
   together now compete for the same stock, something three separate
   shelves could never let happen — pooling trades "wasted spares" for
   "a shared risk of running out."
====

=== Exercise

The provider now waves a new slide: **99.99%**. DC2 is not there yet
— find the next line of the blame table yourself. Run DC2, count the
red-stretch causes from the event logs, and identify the single
biggest remaining contributor. Then make *one* change to the model —
a second utility feed so the generator never carries, a faster
Chill_Pump repair, your pick — and run 200 trials. Did you clear
0.88 hours? What became the new weak link?

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
The blame table's leaders are the ATS transfer gaps
(about 0.4 h/yr) and generator failures while carrying (about 0.4
h/yr) — the same utility outage wearing two hats. Adding a second
utility feed removes both (the generator never starts), taking the
year to roughly 0.5–0.6 h of downtime — about 99.994%, past the
99.99% line. The new weak link is the Chill_Pump: a single, unredundant
block in the cooling path, now responsible for a third of what little
downtime remains. (It is N — and N is always next.)
====

=== What you learned

* Availability targets convert to downtime budgets in hours per year;
  99.98% means 1.6 hours — the number that disciplines every design
  choice.
* N, N+1, and 2N are your old friends the series chain, the k-of-n
  vote, and the parallel pair, wearing industry badges.
* Tuning is the <<ch-weaklink>> loop at facility scale: blame table, fix
  the leader by shrinking *consequence*, re-run, and stop when the
  budget is met — watching yellow grow as red shrinks.

You have come a long way from a camping lantern. Same dice, same
event log, same three numbers — just aimed at bigger machines. Go
point the simulator at something that matters to you.

think, one event at a time. Then one last capstone — the hardest of
